You come off the A1 expecting a hotel and you get a hedge. The turning for Wentbridge is easy to miss, and we nearly did, because there is nothing to see. Then the road drops into the Went Valley and the whole thing appears at once: honey stone, a flying flag, a lawn running away to trees, and a wooded slope rising behind it. Thirty seconds earlier we had been on the Great North Road behind a dusty lorry.

That gap between the road and the valley is the reason this weekend works. West Yorkshire gets written off as the bit you drive through on the way to the Dales, and the county's own tourist copy does not help by leading with shopping arcades and light festivals. What is actually here, within half an hour of that turning, is one of the best sculpture parks in Europe, a Norman castle with a guide who knows it inside out, and the national collection of arms and armour. None of them is more than half an hour from the others. You spend the weekend seeing things and almost no time driving.

![The stone frontage of Wentbridge House Hotel with a flag flying, wooded valley slope rising behind it](/images/wentbridge-house-hotel-exterior-west-yorkshire.jpg)

## Why go

The honest pitch is that this is the best value weekend we have had in the UK, and the hotel is most of the reason why.

Wentbridge House was built in 1700, was once owned by the Bowes-Lyon family, and sits in twenty acres of gardens in the valley bottom. We paid around £170 a night including a cooked breakfast made to order, which for a country house with two restaurants and grounds like that is not a lot. We have paid more for less in worse places. Expect it to climb in school holidays.

Around it, the days sort themselves out. Saturday is the sculpture park, which is a beautiful walk in a proper landscape rather than a gallery with grass. Sunday is the Royal Armouries in Leeds, twenty five to thirty minutes up the road, free to get into, and considerably better than the phrase "museum of weapons" prepares you for. Friday afternoon, if you arrive before your room is ready as we did, is Pontefract Castle, fifteen minutes away and free.

You could do this weekend on a much smaller budget by staying in Wakefield, and if the hotel is the part you would rather not pay for, that is the sensible swap. But the hotel is the part we would pay for again.

![A domed stone rotunda on a striped lawn at Wentbridge House, framed by mature trees](/images/wentbridge-house-gardens-rotunda.jpg)

## What is not worth your Saturday afternoon

The National Coal Mining Museum, and we went in wanting to like it.

It is free, parking was free when we visited in early July, and it sits about ten minutes from the sculpture park, so on paper it is an obvious add-on. The underground tour, led by an ex miner 140 metres down, is the thing the place is actually famous for, but bear in mind if you want to do it, it needs booking ahead - something that we failed to gather before going.

The rest of the site was a disappointment. The buildings hold plenty of original equipment, but the signage was dated and grubby. Every single interactive we tried, the games and the screens, was either switched off or broken. There were no staff anywhere. We walked five minutes over to the stables to see the pit ponies and there were no ponies in them. We left after an hour and laughed about it in the car.

Online reviews are much warmer than this, so it is possible we caught it on a bad day. But we can only tell you what we found, and what we found was a hard pass even at nothing. If you want to go, book the underground tour first and check it is actually running, because that is the part that might be worth the drive - the rest was, sadly, rubbish.

The one genuinely lovely thing we saw there was on the nature trail: a small waterfall running bright ochre through the woods, stained orange by iron in the old mine water. It is a strange, pretty thing and it is free to walk to.

![A small waterfall running rust orange through green woodland](/images/national-coal-mining-museum-nature-trail-waterfall.jpg)

One note on money. Parking is normally free with donations invited, but there is a summer charge: for 2026 it is £10 per car from 18 July to 30 August, payable by cash or card. Worth knowing before you turn in, because £10 to park at a free museum stings.

## What to do

### Yorkshire Sculpture Park

Give it the whole day and you still will not finish it. We arrived about ten, left mid afternoon, and ran out of legs long before we ran out of park.

Book in advance. Ticket check on arrival was nice and quick and saved the hassle. Tickets were £12 each and parking is free. The important part, and it is easy to get wrong: **if you want to see Seizure, you have to select that ticket specifically.** It runs on timed slots with limited capacity, and admission to the park is included in that ticket at no extra cost, so there is no reason not to book it that way.

Seizure is the one you will still be talking about on the drive home, and the good news is that it is permanent, so you can plan a visit around it rather than hope. Roger Hiorns pumped 75,000 litres of liquid copper sulphate into a condemned council flat in south London in 2008 and let it crystallise over the walls, floor, ceiling and bath. When the block was due for demolition the whole thing, over thirty tonnes of it, was cut out of the building and moved here for good, where it now sits inside a purpose built concrete structure that from outside looks like nothing at all, a slightly raised grey box.

Inside it is very tight. Six or seven people including us, and it still felt close. The crystals are a deep glittering blue over every surface, and they come alive when a torch catches them. You are given shoe covers at the door and the staff are friendly about it. Fifteen minutes and you are back out in the daylight, slightly recalibrated.

![A close view of a wall covered in deep blue copper sulphate crystals](/images/roger-hiorns-seizure-copper-sulphate-crystals.jpg)

The lakeside route is the walk to do. It is about five kilometres, on gravel paths, through woodland and across grass, and it took us an hour and a half to two hours going slowly. In July it was fine underfoot. In winter, expect mud. The one thing to know is that the lake sits at the bottom of some steep ground, so the walk back up to the visitor centre and café is a genuine climb at the end of a long, warm afternoon. Manageable, but you will feel it.

The Hepworths are up on the ridge. We found them by following the map from the visitor centre, which is worth picking up rather than trusting your phone.

Then there is the Deer Shelter Skyspace, five minutes downhill from the car park towards the lake. It is a concrete chamber (previously used as shelter for deer in harsh conditions - as the name suggests!) built into the land with a square hole cut in the roof, and you sit on the bench and look at the sky through it. Two other people were in there when we arrived and they left within a minute or two. We stayed about fifteen minutes. It sounds daft written down, but it was the most relaxed either of us felt all weekend.

The other thing nobody warns you about is the sheep. They graze loose across the park and they are entirely unbothered by people, so you can get close, within reason and with a bit of respect. We watched one work its way steadily towards a family's picnic with real intent and a glisten in its eye.

![Sheep lying and grazing on grass at Yorkshire Sculpture Park](/images/yorkshire-sculpture-park-sheep-grazing.jpg)

Lunch in the café was good, and reasonable for somewhere with a captive audience. A burger and chips, a pasta dish, and coffee that was genuinely good rather than merely present. The chocolate cake is the size of a small building and is plainly meant for two. It was not shared.

![A gate opening onto parkland at Yorkshire Sculpture Park with wooded hills beyond](/images/yorkshire-sculpture-park-country-park-view.jpg)

### The Royal Armouries, Leeds

Free, five floors, and we badly underestimated it. Three hours planned, four and a half spent.

**The tip that matters: get the day's show schedule from the front desk when you arrive and plan your visit around the live combat demonstrations.** Watching two people go at each other with longswords in the tiltyard teaches you more about how the things actually worked than an hour of reading labels ever will. It was the best part of the whole weekend, and if you drift round the galleries without checking the times you will miss them entirely.

Two objects to look for. Henry VIII's horned helmet is the museum's own emblem and deserves to be: it has a grimacing face, spectacles and a pair of ram's horns, and it is beautiful and hideous at the same time in a way that is quite hard to look away from. It came from Maximilian I as a gift to Henry in 1511, part of a full suit of armour that was later scrapped, leaving just the head. Then find the Vickers Maxim Mark I, the British Army's main machine gun through the First World War. Sitting quietly in a case, having decided a great deal.

![Henry VIII's horned helmet at the Royal Armouries, a steel face with spectacles, a grimace and curled ram's horns](/images/henry-viii-horned-helmet-royal-armouries-leeds.jpg)

There was a Genghis Khan exhibition on when we went, a small extra charge on top of the free entry, and worth it. It runs until early November 2026, tickets are £9 an adult with under sixteens free, it is on the fourth floor, and about forty five minutes covers it.

There are often other live events. Not long after our visit there was a series of Mongolian horse shows, completely free live demonstrations, so it is worth checking the website to see what is on when you go.

### Pontefract Castle

Fifteen minutes from Wentbridge, free to walk into, and the right thing to do with an awkward afternoon before check in.

Do the Explorer Tour. It runs Friday, Saturday and Sunday at 2pm, costs £3.45 for adults and £2.35 for children, takes about forty five minutes, and stays on level paths so it works with a wheelchair or a pushchair. Between January and March it shifts to 1pm and drops slightly in price, so check before a winter visit. You can book online or pick up what is left from the gift shop on the day.

Book it, and here is why. The ruins are more broken up than we expected, and left to ourselves we would have walked round some walls for twenty minutes and left none the wiser. Our guide was a proper local history enthusiast and he filled in everything that is missing, so by the end you can see the castle that was there rather than the stumps that are. This was once one of the most important castles in the country, known as the Key to the North. Three pounds forty five is the best value on this entire weekend.

There is a small gift shop and a café on site if you are that way inclined, open every day except Monday.

![The stone towers and ruined walls of Pontefract Castle rising above a grassy bank](/images/pontefract-castle-ruins-towers.jpg)

## Where to stay

Wentbridge House, and it is not a close call.

We had the Superior Double, which is effectively the standard room. Spacious, spotless, and a very good bed. The grounds are quiet enough that we both slept properly, which in a hotel a few hundred metres from the A1 is not a given: the valley does the work. Breakfast is cooked fresh to order and was lovely. All in, around £170 a night.

The grounds are twenty acres and we walked maybe a fifth of them, wandering before dinner rather than setting out properly. Trees, meadow, mown lawns, borders. It is a stroll rather than a walk, and it is the right thing to do while you wait for a table.

One thing we did not know until afterwards, and now wish we had. The house is known for its antiques and for its Thompson furniture, made by Robert Thompson's workshop in Kilburn. Thompson signed his work with a small carved mouse, tucked somewhere on almost every piece. We walked past all of it without looking. Do better than we did: check the legs and the edges, and see how many mice you can find.

## Where to eat

You do not need to leave the hotel, and we did not.

**The Brasserie** is the one for the first night. Relaxed, busy, no fuss, and open every day, which the other one is not. Mains run roughly £20 to £30, starters £10 to £15, puddings around a tenner. We ate out on the garden terrace because the evening was warm, and it manages to be elegant and calm at the same time without anyone being stiff about it.

Have the Dovecote Park sirloin. Then have the sticky toffee pudding, which our waitress recommended and which is the house classic. She was right, and if you are the sort of person who feels obliged to try something more interesting, do not.

**The Fleur de Lys** is the proper dining room and it is a completely different evening: a small, quiet space, unhurried service, two AA Rosettes, and cooking that expects you to sit still for a couple of hours. We went at 7 and got up at half nine.

The catch, and it is the thing to plan around: **dinner in the Fleur de Lys is Friday and Saturday only, 7pm to 9pm.** If you arrive on a Sunday you have missed it. It does serve Sunday lunch from noon to 2.30 if your timings work the other way. Starters are around £12 to £17, mains £20 to £35, puddings £10 to £12.

Doing the Brasserie on Friday and the Fleur de Lys on Saturday is the right way round, and not only because of the opening times. You want the relaxed one when you arrive tired, and the long one when you have nothing to get up for.

## The weekend, in order

**Friday.** Drive up in the early afternoon and watch for the turning, because you will not see the hotel until you are on top of it. If the room is not ready, go straight to Pontefract Castle, fifteen minutes away, and catch the 2pm tour. Back by half four. Walk a bit of the gardens. Dinner in the Brasserie.

**Saturday.** At the sculpture park for ten. Do the lakeside route in the morning while your legs are fresh, sit in the Skyspace, find the Hepworths on the ridge, then climb back up for lunch in the café. Seizure in the afternoon on your booked slot. You will still not have seen all of it. Back at the hotel by six, look less like someone who has walked five miles, and be in the Fleur de Lys at seven.

**Sunday.** Check out and drive to Leeds, twenty five to thirty minutes. Park at Citipark Leeds Dock, two minutes' walk from the door: we pre booked five hours online for £7.50, which we would do again. Get the show schedule at the front desk, then give it four hours rather than the three you have planned.

If you would rather swap the Saturday afternoon, the Coal Mining Museum is ten minutes from the sculpture park and free. Read the section above first.

## Pro tips

- **Book the sculpture park ticket that includes Seizure**, not the plain admission. It costs no more and the slots are limited. This is the single easiest thing to get wrong on this weekend.
- **Pre book parking at Citipark Leeds Dock.** Five hours came to £7.50 online. Paying on the day is usually dearer.
- **Get the Royal Armouries show times at the desk, first thing.** The live combat is the best thing in the building and it runs to a timetable you cannot guess.
- **The Fleur de Lys only does dinner on Friday and Saturday.** Build the weekend around that rather than discovering it at six o'clock on a Sunday.
- **Take a proper water bottle for the sculpture park.** Five kilometres in July, and a climb back up to the café at the end, and there is not much shade on the open ground. I always carry a Chilly's, which could not be more worth the money - believe me, family and friends are sick of me banging on about it. - **Wear comfortable shoes.** The lakeside paths are gravel, grass and woodland, fine in summer, and the slope back up is steeper than it looks on the map.
- **Look for the mouse.** Thompson furniture, small carved mouse, somewhere on the piece. We missed every single one.
